Ghost in the Shield
Pursued and dismembered by the corrupt police force, you find a talking prosthetic shield-arm and forge a symbiotic alliance to topple your dystopian city from the ground up, deflecting, attacking and navigating platforms all the way to the top.

This project quickly became one of my favorites, because it marked the move from Unity to The Game Assembly's own C++ engine, TGE. TGE has very few built-in systems, so we had to build many features from the ground up, including collisions and physics.

Working that close to the metal was challenging but rewarding. It forced me to understand how game systems work under the hood and to solve problems I would normally rely on an engine for. It gave me a much stronger grasp of both C++ and engine architecture.

At a glance

  • Team: 14
  • Reference: Katana Zero and Metroid Dread
  • Duration: 11 weeks at 50% pace
  • Engine: TGE, The Game Assembly's C++ engine
  • My contributions: collision system, force engine, player movement and the player state stack

Collision system

My goal was a collision system that was easy for the rest of the team to use. I could reach that goal by building it on top of ES, our component system.

Force engine

I wanted a character controller that felt good and natural, so I wanted it to be physics based. TGE has no physics engine, so I wrote one that only applies forces to objects, hence the name Force Engine.

The force engine also made it easy to separate overlapping colliders: just apply a force that pushes them apart.

Player movement

I wanted movement where the player accelerates smoothly in the direction of input up to a maximum speed. It should feel responsive but natural, with a little inertia when changing direction. When there is no input, the player gradually slows down instead of stopping abruptly, which makes the movement feel fluid and dynamic.
