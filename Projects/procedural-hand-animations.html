FABRIK Hand Animation
During the second year at The Game Assembly we have a specialization course that lets us dive deeper into a topic of our choice. I chose to explore how procedural animation can make gameplay more immersive.

I implemented inverse kinematics with the FABRIK algorithm and used it to animate a skeleton. My goal was to recreate the subtle hand animations in Uncharted 4: A Thief's End, the small movements that add realism and presence. The whole project was built in my own C++ engine, Zengine, using DirectX 11.

At a glance

  • Team: Solo
  • Reference: Uncharted 4
  • Duration: 8 weeks at 50% pace (February – April 2025)
  • Engine: My own engine, Zengine (DirectX 11)
  • Tech: C++, FABRIK, DirectX 11, ImGui, Premake, nlohmann/json

Why FABRIK?

FABRIK is well suited to games. Compared to other inverse kinematics methods it balances efficiency and accuracy, which makes it a good fit for real-time use where performance matters.

It has one big limitation: it only works with positions, so rotations have to be handled separately. That adds complexity for 3D skeletal animation, where orientation matters.

Despite that, FABRIK is one of the simplest IK methods to implement, which made it a practical choice for a short course.

Solving positions

Solving positions is straightforward: run the two passes for a number of iterations. One iteration was enough for my joint chain, but longer chains needed more. Each chain also has a flag that decides whether the target is in the chain's local space or in world space using the model's transform.
  • Backward pass: starts at the end effector and moves toward the root, so the end effector reaches the target while segment lengths stay the same.
  • Forward pass: moves from the root back out, repositioning joints with the root fixed in place, which restores the structure while keeping the target alignment.

Solving rotations

This was the hardest part. I struggled with which space the positions should be calculated in, and which space would make the rotation math easier.

The solution was a SolveRotation step that runs after the positions are updated. It goes through each joint except the last and rotates it to point at the next joint in the chain. For the last joint, the chain stores a parameter where you can set the rotation yourself with Euler angles.

The rotations use quaternions, which keeps them smooth and avoids gimbal lock, so the joints end up with both correct positions and natural orientations.

Procedural animation

I brought the physics engine I made in Project 7 into my own engine. A raycast is cast from the lower right side of the body, and the IK target is set to wherever the ray hits.

Improvements

Right now the ray moves at a fixed distance from the body, which makes the animation feel unnatural. Instead, I want the ray's origin to follow at a fixed distance and gradually correct itself toward the center of the body over time. That would prevent sudden movements and make transitions more fluid.

Conclusion

Working with FABRIK was a fun and rewarding challenge. It gave the character life and added the extra polish that makes animation feel natural and responsive. Some parts were tricky, like figuring out rotations and getting everything into the right space, but seeing the joints move naturally in real time made it worth it. I still want to make the raycasting behave more naturally, but overall I'm really happy with how it turned out.
